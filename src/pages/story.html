So he started drawing — one fly at a time, until there were enough of them to be a deck. Every illustration on every card is original and drawn by hand. Then came the twist that turned a study aid into something people would actually keep: why not make them playing cards too?

That is the part that makes it work. A deck of cards gets dealt, argued over and left out on the table. Fifty-four flies get looked at hundreds of times without anybody ever deciding to sit down and study.

Turning drawings into a real deck came down to one decision the whole product rests on: print it on proper playing card stock. Not board with rounded corners — {{ site.product.stock }}, at {{ site.product.dimensions }}, from {{ site.product.manufacturer }}. It costs more per deck, and it is the difference between a reference card and a deck of cards. A deck of cards gets played with.

Then Audrey got involved

Audrey is Ken’s daughter, and an entrepreneurship graduate of Loyola Marymount University’s College of Business Administration. She handles design direction, brand and community — which is the tidy way of saying she is the reason the drawings became a deck instead of a folder on a laptop.

They are farmers and anglers long before they are a company. The deck is made in {{ site.location.city }}, {{ site.location.regionName }}, and every email that arrives is answered by one of the two of them.

A couple of things we won’t claim

We would love to tell you nobody else makes fly-fishing cards. We can’t — several companies do, some of them good, and one search would catch us out. What we will happily stand behind is narrower and checkable: the art is Ken’s and drawn by hand, the stock is genuine Bicycle from {{ site.product.manufacturer }}, and a set amount from every deck goes to {{ site.conservation.partner }}, who we are already a business member of.

We are not going to invent a founding legend either. There is no golden evening on a riverbank in this story, mostly because there is no photograph of one. There is a man who wanted to keep up with his friends, and his daughter who thought he was onto something.
