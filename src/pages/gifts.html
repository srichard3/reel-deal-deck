Gift guide

Gifts for someone who loves fly fishing

You are not going to out-guess an angler on gear. They have opinions about tippet. The safe gifts are the ones with nothing to size and nothing to match — a day with a guide, a gift card to their shop, small tools they are forever losing. The risky ones are rods, reels, waders and boots. Here are ten that work, and the short list to avoid.

We make one of the ten. It is at number four, priced and rated the same way as the rest, and the three above it cost more than we charge.

What not to buy

Four categories cause nearly every returned fly-fishing gift. They are the expensive ones, which is why most gift guides lead with them.

  • Rods and reels

    A rod is chosen by weight, length and action against the water somebody fishes and the reel they already own. An angler who wants a new one has usually had the exact model in mind for months. This is the most expensive way to get a gift wrong.

  • Waders and boots

    Sized, and sized unusually — stockingfoot waders are bought a size by height and build, and boots go over them rather than on a bare foot. Even people who know the angler's shoe size get this wrong.

  • Anything with a brand loyalty attached

    Lines, leaders, floatant, packs. These look like safe consumables and are not: most anglers have settled on one and will quietly not use another.

  • A fly rod “starter kit” for someone who already fishes

    Fine for a true beginner, and faintly insulting to anyone past their first season. If you are not sure which they are, assume past it.

None of the above is sold here, which is why we can say it plainly. If you want the short version to send to somebody else: if it is sized, or if they would have an opinion about the brand, do not buy it.

The ten

Gifts that work, in order of how hard they are to get wrong

  1. 01

    A day with a guide

    $$$ Hard to get wrong

    The best gift in the sport and the one almost nobody buys themselves, because it feels like an indulgence next to a thing you keep. A half day on water they already fish teaches more than a season of guessing, and on unfamiliar water it is the difference between a trip and a long walk. Book through a fly shop near the water rather than a directory; they know who is good with beginners and who is better with somebody who already casts well.

    Why it is safe: nothing to size, nothing to match, and the date can be left open.

  2. 02

    A gift card to their local fly shop

    $$ Hard to get wrong

    This is the honest answer to everything in the “what not to buy” list above. They pick the line, the leader and the brand they have already decided on, and the money stays with the shop that keeps their river staffed with people who know it. Find the shop nearest the water they fish, not the one nearest their house.

    Why it is safe: it converts every risky gift on this page into a safe one.

  3. 03

    The small tools they keep losing

    $ Hard to get wrong

    Nippers, forceps, a zinger, a hook hone. Every one of these ends up in a river, in a car footwell or in last year's jacket, and nobody has ever been annoyed to own a spare. This is the exception to the brand-loyalty rule: these are cheap enough that a second one is useful even if it is not their usual.

    Why it is safe: consumable, cheap, and genuinely always needed again.

  4. 04

    A deck of fly cards

    $ Hard to get wrong

    Fifty-four classic flies, one to a card, each saying what it imitates. It answers the “what do I tie on” question at the truck, and it is a real deck, so it also plays poker. This is the one we make — ${{ site.pricing.retail.perDeck }} a deck, {{ site.product.origin }} — and it is on this list for the same reason as the other nine: there is no size, no compatibility and no brand to get wrong.

    Why it is safe: useful to a beginner, interesting to somebody thirty years in, and wrong for neither.

  5. 05

    A membership to a conservation group

    $$ Hard to get wrong

    Most anglers mean to join something and never get round to it. A membership in their name puts money into the water they fish and usually comes with a magazine and a local chapter that runs work days and river cleanups. Pick a group that works on their river if you can find one; a national membership is a fine fallback.

    Why it is safe: nothing to size, and it says you were paying attention to what they care about.

  6. 06

    A proper sun hat

    $$ Mostly safe

    Wide brim all the way round, not a cap. Anglers stand in reflected light for hours and most of them own a hat that is too small for the job. Sizing is forgiving — most are one size with a drawcord — but colour and shape are a matter of taste, which is the only reason this is not in the top five.

    Where it goes wrong: they may already have one they love. Keep the receipt.

  7. 07

    A book about fishing rather than about tackle

    $ Mostly safe

    Essays and accounts of places age far better than instruction, and the people who fish most tend to read about it in winter. Instruction is riskier: it dates, and it implies they need teaching. If you know a river they love, a book about that river is close to unbeatable.

    Where it goes wrong: taste, and the chance they own it.

  8. 08

    A print of a fly, a fish or a river

    $$ Mostly safe

    Something for the wall of an office rather than the back of a truck. A plate of a classic fly pattern, or a map or picture of water they know, does what no piece of equipment does: it sits where other people see it and gets asked about.

    Where it goes wrong: entirely a matter of their taste and their walls.

  9. 09

    A fly box

    $$ Easy to get wrong

    A good gift for somebody in their first season or two, who does not yet have a system. For anybody else it is close to hopeless: foam against slit foam against magnetic, size, how it sits in which pocket. Experienced anglers have settled this question and will not re-open it for a present.

    Where it goes wrong: they already have the one they like, and it is the one they like.

  10. 10

    Flies themselves

    $ Easy to get wrong

    The obvious idea and the hardest one on this list. Flies are chosen for a water, a season and a hook size, and the ones an angler needs in April are not the ones they need in August. If you are going to do it anyway — and it is lovely when it lands — buy a small selection of classics rather than guessing at a hatch, and buy them from the shop nearest their water.

    If you are going to try: the Fly-brary lists fifty-five classics and what each one imitates, which is a better starting point than a bargain assortment.

Nothing on the list matches all three answers. Widen one of them — most likely the budget.

Before you decide

What gift-buyers ask us

Will they already have one?

Almost certainly not. There are fly-fishing card sets on the market — they are photo-printed reference cards, sold mostly through shops, and very few anglers own one.

This deck has not been printed yet, so nobody has it. If you get in early you are giving something that did not exist last year, which is exactly the opposite of the problem you have with another net or another pair of nippers.

I do not know anything about fly fishing. Can I still pick this?

Yes, and that is rather the point. You do not have to guess a hook size, a rod weight, a line taper, or a reel that matches the one they already own.

Fifty-four classic flies with what each one imitates is useful to a beginner and interesting to somebody who has fished for thirty years. There is no size to get wrong and nothing to check for compatibility. If you want to sound like you knew what you were doing, read a few entries in our Fly-brary first — it is free and it takes five minutes.

What is a good fly fishing gift under $25?

Small tools they are forever losing — nippers, forceps, a zinger, a hook hone — or a deck of fly cards. Both clear the two tests that matter at this price: nothing to size, and no brand they have already settled on. Flies also sit under $25 but are the hardest thing on this page to buy for somebody else, because the right ones depend on their water and the month.

What do you buy someone who already has all the fly fishing gear?

Stop buying gear. The gifts that land for somebody with a full kit are an experience, a contribution or an object: a day with a guide on water they already fish, a membership to a conservation group working on their river, or something that is not equipment at all. An angler with everything has everything precisely because they choose it themselves.

Is a gift card a cop-out for a fly fisherman?

No, and for tackle it is usually the best answer available. Lines, leaders and packs are exactly the things an angler has already decided about, so a gift card to the fly shop nearest the water they fish buys the right version of the thing you would have bought wrong — and keeps the money with a shop that keeps that river staffed.

What if they do not like it?

It is a deck of cards. Worst case it lives in a boat bag and gets played with on a rained-out afternoon. That is a much softer landing than a rod they have to pretend to like.

Low downside is the entire reason a deck is one of the few genuinely safe things to buy for an expert.
